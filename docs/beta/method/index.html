Abstract

Bacteria tests at the mouth of San Pedro Creek, on Linda Mar beach in Pacifica, California, are taken about once a week, and the results often take weeks to become public. This paper describes a daily forecast meant to fill that gap. It does not predict illness, and it never says the water is safe. It estimates how likely the county's weekly test would be to fail the state standard on a given day, and sorts days into four levels of risk.

This forecast model relies on two things that can be known in advance: how much rain has fallen or is forecast over three days, and how wet or dry the ground is. In the record since 2022, tests failed more often after rain and when the ground was dry.

The forecast was built by following a published method for California beaches (Searcy et al., 2018), with four stated changes: only information available ahead of time is used to make the forecast, only the years since 2022 were used to determine the model, the forecast runs all year and through storms, and levels of risk replace the published method's yes-or-no call. To check it, each year's tests were set aside in turn, the forecast was built from the other four years, and it was then scored on how well it ranked the hidden year's days from lower to higher risk. It scored 0.73 on a scale where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1 is perfect. Guessing from the most recent test result scored 0.55. A wider search across 50 candidate inputs, including temperature, tide and waves, did not do better.

The forecast has real limits. It rests on five years and 243 tests. It does not meet the published method's standard for a yes-or-no advisory, and neither did the most polluted beaches in the original study. This paper describes version 0.1, a first version of the forecast model. Each version is left unchanged while it is being tested, every forecast is logged before the result is known, and the data and code behind it are public so that others can check it and improve it.

1. Why forecast, and why this way

Weekly test results often take weeks to become publicly accessible, and one test describes one moment on one day. A surfer deciding whether to paddle out needs something before a result exists. A forecast can offer that, but only if people can check how it works and how often it is right. So this forecast model uses two inputs that anyone can work out from public rain records, and its equation, its test results and its errors are published in full.

The method it follows. The model follows the procedure of Searcy et al. (2018), the study behind Heal the Bay's NowCast, which issues daily water-quality predictions for California beaches (Heal the Bay, 2020). That study built a separate statistical model for each of ten beaches, covering April to October. Each morning, each model made a yes-or-no call for that same day: “Post” a warning, or “No-Post” (Searcy et al. 2018, pp. 2, 4, 8).

How those models were built. The authors began with over 100 measurable things per beach, such as rain, tide and wave height. Statisticians call these variables. They then used stepwise regression, a procedure that adds variables one at a time and keeps only those that improve the model's match with past bacteria counts. Each finished model kept between 2 and 10 variables (pp. 3-4, 6). Rain was kept in 27 of the 28 models, tide in 22 and waves in 16. The types of variable the study lists include neither groundwater nor soil moisture (p. 6 and its Table 3).

How those models were judged. A model was accepted only if it met three conditions, both on the data used to build it and on data kept back for testing (p. 4). It had to catch more than 30% of failing tests. It had to catch at least 10 percentage points more of them than the “current method”, in which the latest test result decides whether a warning is posted. And it had to correctly clear more than 85% of passing tests. Section 4 explains these measures.

A related study of 25 California beaches found two kinds of model “consistently the best performing”: classification trees, and binary logistic regression with threshold tuning (Thoe et al., 2015). The model in this paper is a binary logistic regression: an equation that turns its inputs into the chance of one of two outcomes, here a failed test or a passed one.

Four stated changes. This model departs from the published procedure in four ways.

  • Only inputs that can be known in advance are used, so that the model can forecast ahead and not only describe the same morning.

  • The model is built from tests since 2022, because results at this beach changed sharply around then (Section 2).

  • The model runs all year and through storms. The published models cover April to October and pause while a rain advisory is in effect.

  • The model gives levels of risk, not a yes or no, because at this beach no yes-or-no model can meet the published pass mark (Section 5).

Section 6 sets each change beside what the original study says.

2. The beach, the tests and the data

San Pedro Creek drains the Linda Mar valley and runs to the surf at Pacifica State Beach, known locally as Linda Mar.

Who tests the water. Three groups do. San Mateo County samples the ocean at the creek mouth (county station 5) and the creek itself, about once a week. Surfrider's San Mateo County chapter has tested the creek mouth since December 2013. The Linda Mar Water Quality Coalition has tested four sites along the creek every week since July 2024.

What the model forecasts. The model forecasts the result of the county's ocean test at the creek mouth. That spot has a long, steady record of public results, notoriously bad water quality (Heal the Bay, 2023-2026), and waves that are popular with surfers. The test counts enterococcus, a bacterium that lives in the gut and is a standard marker of fecal contamination (U.S. EPA, 2012). Counts are reported as a most probable number (MPN) per 100 mL of water. California's standard for a single sample is 104 (California Code of Regulations, title 17, § 7958): a test above it fails. This paper calls a test above 312, three times the standard, “far over”.

The two inputs. The first is rain. No rain gauge in the valley itself has a complete record, so the model uses daily rain for the watershed from ERA5, a widely used weather record that reconstructs past conditions from observations (Hersbach et al., 2020), obtained through the Open-Meteo service (Zippenfenig, 2023).

The second is ground wetness. It is an index, meaning a single number built from other measurements, here from rain alone. It keeps a running tally of recent rain: each day's rain enters a fast store that drains into a slow store, and the slow store loses about 3% of its contents a day. The index therefore rises after storms and falls through dry spells. It stands in for groundwater, because the valley's monitoring wells have not been read since 2016.

On a forecast morning, three things are known: the forecast rain for that day, the measured rain for the two days before, and the index through the day before. The model uses nothing else. Other inputs were tested, among them air and sea temperature, sunshine, waves, the tide, the time of year and recent test results. None improved the forecast enough to be added (Section 5).

Why only tests since 2022. The county's record at this spot goes back to 2000 (1,265 tests) and falls into three eras. The share of tests failing rose from 9% to 35% to 62%.

To see what changed, the model's equation was fitted separately to each era. Fitting means choosing the weights in the equation so that it matches past results as closely as possible. The weight on rain came out about the same in all three eras: rain has affected results in the same way for 26 years. The weight on ground wetness was nil in the first era, small in the second and large in the third.

The same shift shows without any model. On days with no rain (under 1 mm in three days), the share of tests failing on the driest, middle and wettest ground was 4%, 3% and 8% in 2000-2013. It was 32%, 22% and 26% in 2014-2021, and 77%, 49% and 40% since 2022. The rise in failures on dry ground, late in the dry season, is recent.

For that reason the model is fitted to the 243 tests since 2022, of which 150 failed and 61 were far over. Earlier years are used as a check (Section 5).

For the record. The weights are the coefficients of the logistic regression in Section 3, fitted to each era separately. The 90% uncertainty ranges for the rain weight overlap across the three eras. For the ground weight in 2000-2013 the range spans zero (-0.53 to +0.47). The score is the ranking score explained in Section 4, with each year hidden in turn.

3. The model

The model is one equation in two steps. It turns two numbers, rain and ground wetness, into the chance that the day's test fails.

z=2.051+0.770ln(1+R3)−1.363log10(G)
P(fail)=11+e−z

Reading the equation. R3 is the rain, in millimetres, on the day plus the two days before. G is the ground-wetness index through the day before, with any value under 1 set to 1. The terms ln and log10 are logarithms, which compress large numbers: they make each extra millimetre of rain, or each extra step of wetness, count for a little less than the one before.

The first line combines the two inputs into a score, z. The second line converts that score into a chance between 0 and 1, that is, between 0% and 100%. This conversion is what makes the model a logistic regression. The numbers 2.051, 0.770 and -1.363 are the weights, also called coefficients, found by fitting the equation to the 243 tests. The positive weight on rain means more rain raises the chance of a failed test. The negative weight on ground wetness means drier ground raises it.

How certain the weights are. The equation was refitted many times on random re-draws of the 243 tests. Nine times in ten, the weight on rain fell between 0.51 and 1.13, and the weight on ground wetness between -1.87 and -0.98. These are 90% uncertainty ranges. Neither includes zero, so in this record both effects are distinguishable from chance.

“Usual” matches the overall failure rate since 2022. “Lower than usual” has not occurred from July to October since 2022. Even at that level about one test in three fails. None of the levels means the water is safe. The forecast says when the water is less safe and least safe; it never says when it is safe, because nothing here can show that. Some tests do come back within the standard: 93 of the 243 since 2022, or 38%. But no reliable way has been found to forecast which days those will be, and a passing test describes only one sample at one moment.

What “drier ground, higher risk” does and does not mean. The ground reading mostly marks the dry season: the ground is driest in late summer and autumn, when the weather is also warm and the creek is low. It also captures that the same rain does more harm after a dry spell. With 1 to 10 mm of rain, 91% of tests failed on dry ground (11 tests), against 53% on wet ground (17 tests). It is not evidence that dry ground pollutes the water, nor that wet ground carries sewage. Section 7 takes up what the data say about sources.

4. How the model was tested

Any model looks good when judged on the data it was built from. The fair test is how it does on results it has not seen. Two versions of that test are used here.

  • Each year hidden in turn. One year's tests are set aside. The equation is fitted to the other four years and then used to predict the hidden year. This is repeated for each year from 2022 to 2026, and the results are combined. Statisticians call this cross-validation, and the hidden data “held out”.

  • Forward in time. The tests of 2024, 2025 and 2026 are each predicted from earlier years only. This is closer to real use, but leaves less data to build from.

Three measures. The first is used throughout this paper. The other two are the ones Searcy et al. use, and they apply only when a model is made to give a yes-or-no call.

  • Ranking score. Take one failed test and one passed test at random. The ranking score is how often the model gave the failed one the higher chance. A score of 0.5 is a coin flip and 1 is perfect. Its technical name is the area under the ROC curve, or AUC (Hanley and McNeil, 1982). It is reported for “fail” (over 104) and for “far over” (over 312).

  • Sensitivity. Of the tests that failed, the share the model warned about.

  • Specificity. Of the tests that passed, the share the model correctly did not warn about.

Sensitivity and specificity pull against each other: a model that warns every day catches every failure and clears nothing. Following Searcy et al., each model's warning threshold is set to catch as many failures as possible while still clearing more than 85% of passing tests.

The benchmark and the pass mark. The benchmark is what Searcy et al. call the “current method”: the most recent test result decides whether a warning is posted, until the next result arrives. Their pass mark (p. 4) requires sensitivity above 0.30 and at least 0.10 above the current method's, with specificity above 0.85. A model must meet it on the data it was built from and again on data kept back. At the two most polluted beaches in their study the authors relaxed it, with the beach managers' agreement (pp. 4, 6, 9). Here the pass mark is applied strictly, and also in a relaxed form in which sensitivity need only match the current method's.

Running the published recipe on this beach. To see how the published procedure itself performs here, it was run as the paper describes. Two kinds of model were built: stepwise regression, and the paper's second route, a logistic regression whose variables are chosen by cross-validation. Each was built with and without recent bacteria results as inputs, and the two-input model was scored alongside them. The data were divided between building and testing in the paper's ways: at random (75/25, 70/30 and 60/40, 20 times each), and with the last one or two seasons kept back. All of this was done for the paper's own scope (April to October, dry days only) and for this paper's (all year), using tests from 2015 to 2026 and from 2022 onward.

A test set in advance. The model's two inputs were first chosen by hand. To decide whether automatic selection should replace them, a test was written down before it was run. Every candidate input that can be known ahead of time was included, 50 in all (Table 2 lists the groups). Both of the paper's selection methods were used. Each year was hidden in turn, and the whole selection was redone from scratch each time, so that no hidden year could influence what was chosen. An automatically selected model would replace the two-input model only if its ranking score on the hidden years was at least 0.02 higher. That margin is a judgement, not a test of statistical significance (Section 9).

5. Results

The published pass mark is not met, by this model or any other. Across both scopes, both periods and every way of dividing the data, no model passed, strictly or in relaxed form. That includes the two-input model and every model the published recipe produced. Table 1 shows the all-year case for 2022 onward.

The reason is the beach itself. With 62% of tests failing, simply repeating the last result already catches 65% of failures. The best models catch 40% to 45% while correctly clearing 74% to 83% of passing tests, short of the 85% required. The most polluted beaches in the original study were in the same position, which is why its authors relaxed the criteria there. At Cowell Beach, Santa Cruz, the 2017 nowcast scored sensitivity 0.73 and specificity 0.67, against 0.64 and 0.73 for the current method (their Table 4). This is why the forecast gives graded levels of risk and not a yes or no.

Air temperature, sea temperature and waves were never chosen. Rain on the previous day and the time of year were chosen most consistently, and a falling tide at 09:00 in about half the rounds. To mark the dry season, the automatic selection used the time of year and not ground wetness; it never chose the ground-wetness index of the two-input model. The two carry much the same seasonal signal. The model with ground wetness did better on hidden years, so the choice between them rests on a small difference.

Earlier years as a check. A model fitted to earlier years ranks the 2022-2026 days almost as well as the current one (0.69 to 0.72, against 0.73). But its stated odds are far too low. Fitted to 2000-2013, 2014-2021 or 2000-2021, it predicted on average that 9%, 33% or 18% of recent tests would fail; 62% did. It would also have placed 84% to 99% of recent tests at the lowest level.

Combining all years into one fit does the same (average predicted 25% or 43%; ranking score 0.72). Adding a single extra term that switches on from 2022 repairs the odds and leaves the ranking score at 0.72. But it separates the levels less well, with about half of tests landing in the middle level against a quarter, and it assumes the change of 2022 will last. So the model is fitted from 2022, and earlier years serve as a check.

A longer record, and the tide. The test set in advance was repeated on the record from 2015 to 2026. Once every model was allowed the “since 2022” term, automatic selection again did not beat the two-input model (0.709 for stepwise and 0.706 for logistic, against 0.719). Without that term the stepwise model did better (0.682 against 0.634), because its extra inputs absorbed the change between eras. On recent years alone it tied or lost.

One finding grew firmer on the longer record. A falling tide at 09:00 was chosen in all 12 rounds by both methods without the 2022 term, and in 11 or 12 with it, each time pointing to more failures. It was therefore tested as a third input under the same 0.02 margin. It raised the ranking score from 0.726 to 0.735, short of the margin, so it was left out. It remains a candidate to test again when more years exist.

Other checks.

  • Could the score be luck? Shuffling the results among the days 500 times never produced a score above 0.62, against the real 0.73.

  • What does each input add? Rain alone scored 0.58 and ground wetness alone 0.63. Adding air temperature, the season or winter rainfall did not help.

  • Do past test results help? Searcy et al. used earlier results only where no good model existed without them, and only one of their 28 models kept one (pp. 4, 6). Here the last result on its own scores 0.55. Adding the average of the county tests posted 4 to 10 weeks earlier, which is what is public on a forecast morning, lifts the model from 0.726 to 0.739. Adding the county creek's last four results, as if they were available at once, lifts it to 0.738. Both gains are under the 0.02 margin. A fresh result shifts the odds for the next test by about 10 percentage points for two weeks, and by almost nothing after that. Recent results are therefore shown beside the forecast for reference and are not an input.

6. Where this goes beyond the published method

The model follows the published procedure for choosing and judging its inputs. It differs from the original study in the four ways stated in Section 1. Each is set here beside what that study says.

  • All year, including storms. The study models April to October (p. 2) and makes no prediction while a rain advisory is in effect (pp. 4-5). This model runs all year and through storms, because the worst results at this beach come with winter storms.

  • Days ahead. The study's call is for the same morning (pp. 4, 8). It names forecasting ahead, and prediction in wet weather, as future work (p. 10). The same authors later built forecasts with up to three days' lead time at two California sites (Searcy and Boehm, 2023). How well this model does one to three days ahead has not yet been tested.

  • Levels of risk. The study's output is “Post” or “No-Post”. At this beach a yes-or-no call cannot meet the study's pass mark (Table 1), so the model gives four levels, each with a measured failure rate.

  • Built from 2022 onward. The study notes that trends from year to year, and changes to infrastructure, are reasons models fail when tested (p. 2), and finds that a “one size fits all” approach was not possible (p. 9). Here the record falls into three eras (Section 2), and the model is built from the latest.

7. What the pattern suggests about sources

The forecast says nothing about where the bacteria come from. But the same data bear on that question. One long-standing theory is that leaking private sewer lines in the valley feed the creek. The theory has four links. Each is graded here by what this data can and cannot show.

(a) The problem does not need rain. Strong. Since 2022, 100 of the 150 failed ocean tests (67%) came with under 1 mm of rain in the previous three days. So did 78 of 119 failed tests at the county's creek station (66%), and 94 of 133 at Surfrider's creek-mouth site (71%). Most days are rain-free (73% of ocean tests), and 56% of the tests taken on them failed, against about 4% in 2000-2013. The effect of rain has not changed in 26 years (Section 2), so this rise is the record's clearest sign that a source independent of rain has grown.

(b) It enters the creek in the residential valley. Supported, by the Coalition's own data. In the Coalition's tests from July 2024 to September 2026, the typical dry-weather enterococcus level was 212 at Linda Mar Bridge and 218 at Sanchez Adobe, then 363 at Peralta Bridge and 277 at the creek mouth. “Typical” here is a geometric mean, an average suited to counts that range over factors of ten. The jump between Sanchez Adobe and Peralta Bridge rests on under two years of tests.

(c) It is human waste. The Coalition's result, not tested here. The Coalition reports that DNA testing shows human waste. This paper holds no DNA data and can neither confirm nor challenge that.

(d) It moves with the water table. Suggested by the data; more evidence is needed. On dry days since 2022, tests failed more often when groundwater appeared to be above normal for the date than when it was below: 65% against 54% at the ocean station, 56% against 38% at the county's creek station, and 64% against 52% at the creek mouth. “Groundwater” here is a stand-in: the dry-weather flow of neighbouring creeks.

The evidence so far is limited, and more would be needed to establish this link, for four reasons. It covers about five years. The creeks are down the coast, not San Pedro Creek itself. The wells once used to check the stand-in stopped being read in 2016. And when each week is compared with what is normal for the time of year, no station shows a clear effect. At the county's creek station, a doubling of dry-weather flow goes with results 1.43 times higher, but the 90% uncertainty range runs from 0.97 to 1.72, which includes no effect at all.

Warmth. Failures rise in the warm months, but those are also the dry months, so the two are hard to tell apart. Comparing each week with what is normal for the time of year, a week 3 °C warmer than normal goes with results 0.91 times as high at the ocean station, 1.37 times as high at the county's creek station (range 0.96 to 1.95), and no different at the creek mouth. Warmth may matter in the creek, shows no effect at the ocean station, and is not part of the model.

Other explanations. The data in this section can show that a source independent of rain exists, and roughly where it enters the creek. They cannot show what that source is. Leaking sewer lines are one explanation. Temporary encampments of unhoused people lacking sewer infrastructure near the creek are another that has been suggested. Encampments are a more recent development, and this paper has no record of where they were or when, so it cannot test that explanation. Evidence of human waste, as in link (c), would not separate the two, since both are human sources.

8. Limits

  • It does not meet the published pass mark. No model does at this beach (Section 5). Its levels are rough odds, not precise ones: days given a 45% to 55% chance failed only 23% of the time (26 tests).

  • The inputs were first picked by hand. The two inputs were chosen with all 243 tests in view. They then survived the automatic selection; they did not emerge from it, and the margin was small (Section 5). Scores on hidden years are therefore somewhat flattering.

  • Five years, one era. The model rests on about 240 tests. Its ranking score has a 90% uncertainty range of 0.67 to 0.78, and the selection results rest on the same five years. The effect of ground wetness belongs to the period since 2022: it is weak in 2014-2021 and absent before. If the beach changes again, the second input may stop working. Only the effect of rain has held for the whole record.

  • Thin experience at the extremes. Only one of the fitted tests had more than 75 mm of rain in three days, though the record since 1998 reaches 163 mm. Only five had more than 10 mm of rain on ground drier than index 50, all of which failed, and none on ground drier than index 25. The model's low odds for very wet ground with no rain (index over 150) rest on 9 tests, 4 of which failed, against an average stated chance of 27%.

  • Rain forecasts and lead time. The tests in this paper used measured rain, not forecast rain. When the day's rain was deliberately made wrong at random (amounts off by a factor of two, a quarter of light showers missed, stray showers added), the ranking score fell to 0.71 and the level changed on 10% of days. How well the model does one to three days ahead is untested.

  • The ground index is a stand-in. It is built from rain alone. The valley's wells stopped being read in 2016. Over the years when both exist, the index tracks the wells with a correlation of 0.75, where 1 would be a perfect match.

  • One spot, one test, weeks late. The model forecasts the result of a county test at the creek mouth, not illness. Results become public weeks after the sample. Two labs sampling the same reach on the same morning agree on pass or fail 80% of the time.

  • The lowest level still fails. On “lower than usual” days about one test in three fails (36%).

9. An invitation to build on this work

Version 0.1 is a starting point. It is published with its data, its code and its limits so that others can check it, challenge it and improve it. That invitation is open to anyone: people who surf and swim at Linda Mar, the groups who test the water, students, and researchers who build forecasts like this for other beaches.

Where a better forecast might come from. The work so far points to several leads. None has yet earned a place in the model. Each might, with more data or a better measurement.

How an improvement is judged. In the same way as the present model: on years hidden from the fitting, and from now on against the daily log as well. A change is adopted if it raises the ranking score on hidden years by at least 0.02, the margin set in advance for this paper. That margin is a judgement about how large a gain justifies a more complex model. It is not a test of statistical significance: with 243 tests, differences of this size are within the noise. The choice matters in one case. At 0.01, one more input would have been added: the average of the county tests posted 4 to 10 weeks earlier, which raised the score by 0.013. At 0.03 nothing would change. For later versions the margin will again be set before any new results are examined, and a gain will also have to show in the daily log. Changes are judged using the weather that actually occurred, so that an error in the rain forecast is not mistaken for an error in the model. The model could be withdrawn or rebuilt if a year of logged forecasts shows that it does no better than the last test result, or that its levels do not separate results.

While a version is in use, its inputs, weights and level cut-offs do not change, so its results can be judged fairly. Each day's forecast is recorded before the test it forecasts and later compared with the result. Each improvement is released as a new, named version and tested the same way.

How to take part.

  • Check the numbers. The model, its data and its main test are public in the project's repository, github.com/augers-co/pacifica-beach-water. Its open-data folder holds the data and a guide to three ways of checking: in a spreadsheet, with one command, or from the whole database. A fresh copy of the repository reproduces version 0.1 exactly, using the script pipeline/forecast_rule.py.

  • Propose a change or report a problem. Open an issue on the repository. Say what was run, what came out and what was expected. A wrong number, a better input or a disagreement with the approach are all welcome.

  • Bring a record this paper lacks. A rain gauge in the valley, measurements of the creek, or faster test results would each make one of the leads above testable.

  • Try the method somewhere else. This model should not be carried to another beach. Searcy et al. found that a “one size fits all” approach was not possible, even for beaches close together, and that each beach needs a model built from its own record (p. 9). Later work has begun to test how well models built at some beaches predict results at others (Elahi et al., 2025). What can be reused is the procedure in this paper and its code: gather a beach's public test results and weather records, let the inputs be chosen from that record, and test the result on years held back.

The documents docs/inferences.md and docs/data-sources.md in the repository define each derived quantity and list every source. Forecasts have been logged daily in the open-data folder since 19 November 2026.

This paper was produced with substantial help from AI models made by Anthropic, used through Claude Code. The analysis and writing were done in November 2026, chiefly with Claude Fable 5.2, with Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Claude Opus 5.5 for delegated tasks. The project's data pipeline was built from August 2026 with earlier versions, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Opus 4.8.

The author set the questions, chose the method to follow, agreed the test and its margin before the results were known, decided what the forecast may and may not claim, and reviewed and edited every section.

AI models can make errors that read as confident, in code and numbers as well as in prose. The model, its fit and its main test can be regenerated by anyone from the public code and data.
